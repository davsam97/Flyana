## Three axes, three motions

An aircraft rotates about its centre of gravity around three axes:

| Axis | Motion | Control surface | Pilot's control |
|---|---|---|---|
| longitudinal (nose to tail) | **roll** | **ailerons**, at the wings' trailing edges | stick left or right |
| lateral (wingtip to wingtip) | **pitch** | **elevator**, on the horizontal tail | stick forward or back |
| vertical | **yaw** | **rudder**, on the fin | pedals |

Each control surface is a small hinged panel that locally changes the camber, and so the lift, of part of the aircraft. Being far from the centre of gravity, that small force creates a large **moment** that rotates the aircraft.

- **Ailerons** move in opposite directions: the one going down raises its wing's lift, the one going up lowers it. The aircraft rolls.
- **The elevator** changes the horizontal tail's lift, and so the attitude and the wing's angle of attack.
- **The rudder** pushes the tail sideways and swings the nose.

## Adverse yaw

When the pilot banks to turn right, the left aileron goes down. The left wing lifts more, but **also drags more** (induced drag). It falls back, and the nose first swings… to the left, away from the turn: that is **adverse yaw**. The pilot corrects it with the pedals, "coordinating" ailerons and rudder. A ball in a curved tube shows whether the flight is balanced.

## Other moving surfaces

- **Trim tabs** cancel the stick force for a given speed: the pilot does not have to hold the control all the time.
- **Flaps and slats** add lift at low speed, for take-off and landing (see module 1).
- **Spoilers** rise from the upper surface to destroy lift and add drag, in descent or on landing. On airliners they also help the ailerons roll the aircraft.

## From cables to electrical signals

On a light aircraft, cables and rods link the stick straight to the control surfaces. Large aircraft add hydraulic actuators. Since the Airbus A320, airliners often have **fly-by-wire** controls: the stick sends an order to computers, which drive the surfaces and protect the aircraft against stalling or overstressing.
