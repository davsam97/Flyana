Four forces act on an aircraft in flight. Understanding how they balance means understanding almost all of its performance.

| Force | Direction | Origin |
|---|---|---|
| **Weight** $W = m\,g$ | toward the Earth's centre, applied at the centre of gravity | gravity |
| **Lift** $L$ | perpendicular to the relative wind | the wing |
| **Drag** $D$ | parallel to the relative wind, against the motion | the air on the whole aircraft |
| **Thrust** $T$ | along the engines' axis | the engines |

## Steady level flight

In **straight and level flight at constant speed**, acceleration is zero, so the forces cancel in pairs:

$$
L = W \qquad T = D
$$

A 70-tonne airliner must therefore produce about $70{,}000 \times 9.81 \approx 690$ kN of lift in cruise. The thrust needed is far smaller: $W$ divided by the lift-to-drag ratio, about 40 kN if $L/D = 17$.

## Climbing and descending

In a steady climb along a path angle $\gamma$, part of the weight works against the motion:

$$
T - D = W \sin\gamma
$$

It is the **excess thrust** $T - D$ that lets the aircraft climb. In a **glide**, with no thrust, a component of the weight drives the aircraft forward instead. The descent angle follows $\tan\gamma = D/L$: a glider with a lift-to-drag ratio of 40 covers 40 km for every kilometre of height lost, with no engine at all.

**Example.** An aircraft with $L/D = 15$ loses its engine at 1,000 m. In still air it can cover about $1{,}000 \times 15 = 15$ km before reaching the ground.

## Turning and the load factor

To turn, the pilot **banks** the aircraft by an angle $\varphi$: lift tilts with the wings. Its horizontal component pulls the aircraft into the turn; its vertical component $L\cos\varphi$ must still balance the weight. So **more** lift is needed than in straight flight. That extra is measured by the **load factor**:

$$
n = \frac{L}{W} = \frac{1}{\cos\varphi}
$$

At 60° of bank, $n = 2$: passengers feel twice as heavy, they are said to pull "2 g". Stall speed grows as $\sqrt{n}$: it is multiplied by $\sqrt{2} \approx 1.41$ at 60°. That is why a steep turn at low speed is dangerous.
