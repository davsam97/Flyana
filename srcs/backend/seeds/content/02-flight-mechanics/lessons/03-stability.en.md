A **stable** aircraft returns by itself towards its equilibrium when a gust disturbs it. An **unstable** one drifts further and further away. There are two levels:

- **static stability**: the immediate tendency. Does a restoring force appear when the aircraft leaves equilibrium?
- **dynamic stability**: what happens next. Do the oscillations die out?

A marble at the bottom of a bowl is statically stable. On top of a bump, it is unstable.

## Longitudinal stability (in pitch)

It all comes down to where the **centre of gravity** (CG) sits relative to the **neutral point**, the point where the extra lift of the wing and tail together acts when the angle of attack increases.

- **CG ahead of the neutral point**: a gust pitches the nose up, the extra lift acts behind the CG and brings the nose back down. The aircraft is stable. The distance between the two points, divided by the chord, is the **static margin**.
- **CG behind the neutral point**: the same gust pitches the nose up even more. The aircraft is unstable and very hard to fly, except by computers.

That is why the **balance** (the position of the CG, which depends on loading) must stay between two limits:

- **too far aft**: the aircraft becomes barely stable, even unstable, and recovering from a stall or a spin becomes hard;
- **too far forward**: the aircraft is very stable but "heavy" in pitch. The elevator may run out of authority to raise the nose for take-off or to flare for landing, and stall speed increases, since the tail has to push down.

## Lateral and directional stability

- **Dihedral**, wings tilted up in a V, gives stability in **roll**. If the aircraft banks and slips towards the low wing, that wing sees a larger angle of attack, lifts more and rights the aircraft. A high wing or a swept wing has a similar effect.
- **The fin** gives stability in **yaw**, like the feathers of an arrow or a weathervane: if the nose swings off, the air pushes on the fin and lines it up again.

## Dynamic modes

Even when stable, a disturbed aircraft oscillates for a while. Engineers name these oscillations:

- **the short-period mode**: a few seconds, heavily damped;
- **the phugoid**: a slow oscillation, one to two minutes for an airliner in cruise, in which the aircraft trades **speed and altitude** (climbing as it slows, then descending as it speeds up) at an almost constant angle of attack;
- **Dutch roll**: a mix of roll and yaw, which swept-wing aircraft damp with an automatic **yaw damper**;
- **the spiral mode**: a slow tendency to bank more and more, which pilots correct without even thinking about it.

Modern fighters are sometimes made unstable on purpose to gain manoeuvrability: only their flight computers, correcting tens of times per second, make them flyable.
